Chai Jegop started with my own practice notes. After working on piano and rhythm, I’d tell ChatGPT what I’d done that day, where I kept losing the beat, and what I wanted to try next. Around then, ChatGPT kept recommending a feature called Sites. It came up so often I wondered if it was new. Then ChatGPT suggested turning my notes into a web app, so I gave it a try.

While I was picking a name, I looked up a few words that sounded like **chai**. In Thai, **châi châi (ใช่ ๆ)** is like saying “Yeah, exactly.” There’s also the Hebrew **chai (חַי)**, pronounced closer to *khai*. It means “alive” and can be used to say “I’m living.” In my own words, **“just living.”** In Korean, *chai* means “difference,” and *jegop* means “squared.” I liked putting them together to mean that **the small differences you make in practice add up to a bigger difference over time.**

Practice logs remind me of something from when I used to teach. My students were barely practicing, so I had them keep a log. I even made the whole template for them. One section asked what they’d listened to that day and how it made them feel. One student put down Kwon Jin-ah’s “운이 좋았지” and wrote underneath:

> It felt like being lucky in love…?

It read like a kid scrambling to write a week’s worth of diary entries the night before handing them in to the teacher. And all I could think was, “That’s not even what the song is about.” Looking back, I might’ve just given that student one more blank to fill in.

For my own practice, I wanted to write down something I’d actually come back to a few days later. If all I put is “piano, 20 minutes,” I probably won’t remember what I worked on. “Played straight and shuffle grooves with the click on beats two and four. Next time, record four bars of each and compare them.” That gives me somewhere to start when I sit back down at the keys.

I made the app public after seeing students in an open group chat talk about what they were struggling with while preparing for music school auditions. I thought the log I’d built for myself might help somebody there. I was fine with people seeing my own notes, so I included four real piano and rhythm sessions as samples. When I hadn’t written down how long I practiced, I left it marked “Duration not logged.”

From my experience teaching and running lessons, I can think of students this might suit. If I teach again, I’d like to try it with a student who’d find it useful. At the end of the lesson, we could write down one thing to try at home. If they get stuck, they can leave a quick note, and we can pick up right there at the next lesson. For now, I’m trying it out on myself.
